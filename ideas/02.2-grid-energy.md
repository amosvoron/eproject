## Bottom line

The best entry in sector B is **operating sites under flexible grid connection agreements**. The EU Grids Package of Dec 2025 pushes these agreements as the main way to connect new load and storage faster. Every site that signs one must forecast and obey curtailment limits. Two follow-on problems complete the shortlist: **low-voltage grid visibility for distribution operators** and **demand-side flexibility for industrial loads**. Battery trading and virtual power plants are crowded, so they are left out.

## 2. The shortlisted problems in detail

### B1 — Operating under flexible connection agreements

- **Who pays today.** Under a flexible connection agreement, a grid user connects early but may use its connection only when capacity is available **[web]**. Owners of batteries, EV charging hubs, factories and data centres accept these agreements to skip the queue. They need software that predicts when they will be curtailed and keeps them within the limits.
- **Why demand is growing.** The Commission published the Grids Package on 10 Dec 2025. It makes flexible connection agreements the main tool for faster access, and it replaces "first-come, first-served" with "first-ready, first-served" **[web]**. At least 15 Member States already have connection queues at distribution level **[web]**. In Germany, transmission operators held about 650 requests for large batteries at the end of 2024, totalling about 226 GW **[web]**.
- **Why existing solutions fail.** National designs differ in duration, operating limits and tariffs **[web]**. Generic energy-management software does not model each operator's curtailment rules **[assumed]**.
- **What AI changed.** Forecasting site-level curtailment needs a model of grid conditions, weather and the operator's behaviour. Time-series models trained across many sites can now do that cheaply **[assumed]**.
- **The asset after five years.** Curtailment histories per grid area, and an encoding of each operator's agreement terms. A new entrant cannot buy either.

### B3 — Low-voltage visibility for distribution operators

- **Who pays today.** Distribution operators cannot see their low-voltage networks in real time. Smart-meter data exists, but most operators do not use it for congestion forecasting **[reviewer]**.
- **Why demand is growing.** The Commission's Digitalisation and AI in Energy roadmap of 3 Jun 2026 makes faster deployment of smart meters and AI grid tools one of its three priorities. It sets aside about €265M for research and innovation **[web]**.
- **The risk.** Distribution operators buy slowly. AI.grids, the Commission's sovereign grid-AI project, has 48 partners and may supply part of this work as public models **[web]**. That could become a competitor or a channel.
- **The asset after five years.** Network models trained on topology and meter data from each operator.

### B5 — Industrial demand-side flexibility

- **Who pays today.** Energy-intensive plants pay grid fees and energy prices that reward flexibility. Most plants cannot shift process loads without risk to production **[reviewer]**.
- **Why it fits.** It is the same buyer as sector D (`02.4`), with process data on the same machines. It also acts as the bridge from B1 into industrial sites.
- **Who already competes.** Sympower in the Netherlands aggregates industrial flexibility **[web]**. The 2026 European rounds found were mostly for batteries and virtual power plants: Hybrid Greentech raised about €15M and Engrate €2.5M, while Gridcog sells simulation tools **[web]**.

## 3. Entry decisions

- **First buyer.** Owners of batteries, charging hubs and industrial sites who sign flexible connection agreements. They decide faster than grid operators, so they go first. Distribution operators come later, through B3.
- **First countries.** The Netherlands, because its grid congestion is severe and public **[reviewer]**. Then Germany, where a national grid package with a flexible-connection model was still under negotiation in Jun 2026 **[web]**.
- **Regulatory clock.** The Grids Package dates from Dec 2025, and national implementation varies. The energy roadmap dates from Jun 2026 and is a policy communication without binding force **[web]**. The first data-centre sustainability labels come in 2027 **[sourced]**.
- **Competitive response.** Battery optimisers may add curtailment forecasting. The defence is to cover every asset type and to encode each operator's rules **[assumed]**.
- **Funding.** Horizon Europe Cluster 5 and the roadmap's €265M research envelope **[web]**. The AI.grids community is a possible consortium entry point. Energy VCs are active, as the 2026 rounds above show.
- **Manifest check.** All four requirements pass. The product is European and supports energy autonomy. It has EU and VC funding routes. It is software only with a data moat, and it never owns batteries or cooling hardware.

## 4. Shortlist for the product phase

1. **B1:** a curtailment-forecasting and compliance layer for sites under flexible connection agreements.
2. **B5:** industrial demand-side flexibility, shared with sector D.
3. **B3:** low-voltage visibility for distribution operators, as the long-term expansion.
